Wine is an alcoholic beverage made from the fermentation of grapes, although other fruits can also be used. The production of wine, known as winemaking or vinification, involves harvesting ripe grapes, crushing them to release juice, fermenting the juice with yeast, and then aging the resulting liquid in barrels or tanks.

The history of wine dates back thousands of years. Archaeological evidence suggests that viticulture and wine production originated in regions of Western Asia, with the earliest known wine traces found in what is now Georgia, Iran, and Armenia. Wine quickly became an important cultural, religious, and economic product, spreading through the Mediterranean and Europe.

Different types of wine are created depending on the grape variety, fermentation process, and aging methods:

Red wine is made from dark-colored grape varieties and often fermented with the grape skins to extract color, tannins, and flavor.

White wine is usually made from green or yellow grapes, and fermentation typically occurs without the skins.

Rosé wine combines elements of both red and white winemaking, often by limiting skin contact.

Sparkling wine is carbonated through natural fermentation in bottles or tanks, with Champagne being the most famous example.

Wine has significant cultural and social importance. It is commonly consumed during meals, celebrations, and religious ceremonies. The taste, aroma, and quality of wine are influenced by terroir—the combination of soil, climate, grape variety, and winemaking techniques.

Modern wine production has expanded globally, with renowned regions including France, Italy, Spain, the United States, and Australia. Each region offers distinct styles and flavors, making wine a complex and diverse subject for enthusiasts and professionals alike.

Wine is also a frequent subject in culinary studies, economics, and history, and it has been referenced in literature, art, and philosophy throughout human history.
